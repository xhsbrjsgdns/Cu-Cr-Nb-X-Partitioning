Experimental Investigation of Phase Equilibria in the Cu–Cr–Ti
System
J. H. Li1,2 • X. M. Huang1,2 • L. L. Zhu3 • G. M. Cai1,2
Submitted: 3 February 2021 / in revised form: 24 April 2021 / Accepted: 27 April 2021 / Published online: 29 June 2021
 ASM International 2021
Abstract To provide a basic guide for the design of Cu–
Cr-based alloys, phase equilibrium relations in the Cu–Cr–
Ti system have been studied using diffusion triples and
typical equilibrated alloys. Based on the results from
electron-probe microscopy analysis and x-ray diffraction,
isothermal sections of the Cu–Cr–Ti ternary system at 1073
and 973 K were constructed, wherein 9 and 11 three-phase
equilibria were determined, respectively. A ternary phase
with an approximate composition of (Cu, Cr)4Ti3 was
detected and named as s1. The binary Cu4Ti3 phase does
not connect with s1 in the ternary region. Solid solution
phases (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 and s1 were identiﬁed and conﬁrmed in
both diffusion triple and alloy samples. The solubility of Cr
in Cu3Ti2 extends to 12 at.% at both 1073 and 973 K. (Cu,
Cr)3Ti2 and Cu3Ti2 form a continuous solid solution at
1073 K while do not exist as one stable phase at 973 K. In
addition, two two-phase regions of Cu4Ti ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2
and Cu4Ti3 ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 transform to the three-phase
regions of Cu4Ti ? Cu4Ti3 ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 as the temper-
ature decreases from 1073 to 973 K.

equilibrated alloys  phase equilibria
1 Introduction
Owing to their exceptional combination of high electrical
conductivity, thermal conductivity, excellent resistance to
arc erosions and weld ability,[1, 2] Cu–Cr alloys are widely
used in aerospace, power lead frame materials, and heat
exchange equipment.[3–5] As one of precipitation strength-
ened alloys, the enhanced mechanical properties of Cu–Cr
alloys depend on ﬁnely dispersed Cr-rich phases precipi-
tated in Cu matrix during the aging process. Due to the
extremely low solubility of Cr in Cu, about 0.8 at.% at the
eutectic temperature,[6] only a small fraction of precipitates
form during aging, which limits the strength of Cu–Cr
alloys. Furthermore, Cr particles easily coarsen at elevated
temperature, resulting in poor softening resistance of Cu–Cr
alloys.[7, 8] Therefore, tertiary alloying elements are being
explored and added to Cu–Cr alloys to improve their
mechanical properties. For instance, titanium, which has a
relatively high solubility in Cu matrix, is commonly used to
enhance strength. More importantly, the segregation of Ti
atoms in the periphery of the Cr precipitates suppresses the
coarsening of Cr particles in Cu–Cr–Ti alloys.[9, 10] In
addition, similar to the beneﬁts of Nb and Zr in Cu–Cr
alloys, Ti addition can form an AB2 type intermetallic phase
with Cr, which can serve as a strengthening phase.[11] From
the view of composition and heat-treatment selection, phase
relationship of the Cu–Cr–Ti system is important to the
design of novel high strength copper alloys.

According to the Cu–Ti phase diagram evaluated by
Murray,[19] there exist six stable intermetallic compounds,
i.e. Cu4Ti, Cu2Ti, Cu3Ti2, Cu4Ti3, CuTi, and CuTi2.
Thermodynamic assessments of the Cu–Ti system have
been performed by many groups,[19–21] in these assess-
ments, stable phases Cu3Ti2 and Cu2Ti were not consid-
ered. Kumar et al.[22] re-assessed Cu–Ti system and
considered all the phases and the non-stoichiometry of
CuTi and Cu4Ti. More recently, based on the description of
Kumar et al. and the experimental data from Colinet
et al.,[23] the description for Cu2Ti was revised by Wang
et al.[24].
For the Ti–Cr system, Murray[25] critically reviewed the
phase
equilibrium
relationships
based
on
previous
work.[26–28] There is only one stoichiometric TiCr2 com-
pound. Cuff et al.[28] ﬁrstly reported three allotropes (a, b,
c) of TiCr2, i.e. the low temperature aTiCr2 phase with a
cubic C15 polytype, the intermediate temperature bTiCr2
phase with a hexagonal C36 polytype, and the high tem-
perature cTiCr2 phase with a hexagonal C14 polytype. This
discovery was subsequently conﬁrmed in various binary
and ternary systems proposed by Stein et al.[29]. Recently,
new thermodynamic descriptions regarding the Ti–Cr
system were reported by Pavlu˚ et al.[30] and Cupid et al.,[31]
in which the stability sequence of C15–C36–C14 was
proposed through analysis of structure energy and stability
of Laves phase using an ab initio method.
So far, phase equilibrium data of the Cu–Cr–Ti ternary
system is very limited in literature, and no ternary inter-
metallic phase data for this system has been reported.
Crystal structure data[31, 32] of the solid phases in the Cu–
Cr–Ti system are summarized in Table 1. The generally
accepted phase relations for the binary systems Cu–Cr,[18]
Ti–Cr,[31] and Cu–Ti[24] are shown in Fig. 1. The present
work intends to experimentally determine the 1073 and
973 K isothermal sections of the Cu–Cr–Ti system using
diffusion triples. Additional equilibrated alloy samples
were also prepared to conﬁrm the results obtained from
diffusion triples and further complete the phase equilibrium
relations for this system.
2 Experimental method
Owing to the high efﬁciency, diffusion triples were fabri-
cated to investigate the phase equilibria in the Cu–Cr–Ti
system. The procedure to fabricate the diffusion triples is
schematically shown in Fig. 2. The starting pure metal
bricks of Cu, Cr, and Ti all have a purity of 99.99% (in
wt%). The dimensions of Cu, Cr and Ti bricks were 6 mm
9 5 mm 9 5 mm, 5 mm 9 5 mm 9 5 mm, and 5 mm 9
5 mm 9 5 mm, respectively. The Cu and Ti bricks were
ﬁrst clamped together using a custom-built ﬁxture. Then,
the fabricated diffusion couple was annealed in a vacuum
furnace at 1073 K for 8 h to promote diffusion between Cu
and Ti bricks. After that, the Cu–Cr–Ti diffusion triple was
made by bonding the prefabricated Cu–Ti diffusion couple
and Cr brick. Subsequently, the diffusion triple was
encapsulated in an evacuated quartz tube backﬁlled with
pure argon and annealed at 1073 and 973 K for 30 and 60
days, respectively. After annealing, the quartz tubes were
quickly taken out of the furnaces and quenched in ice water
by breaking the tubes. Finally, the diffusion triples were
mounted, ground, polished and then analyzed by electron-
probe microscopy analysis (EPMA).
Based on the preliminary experimental results obtained
from the diffusion triples, a set of Cu–Cr–Ti alloys with
their compositions carefully selected were further prepared
by arc-melting on a water-cooled copper plate under argon
atmosphere. During the melting, the alloy samples were
turned over and re-melted at least three times to ensure
homogeneity. The alloys were evaluated to ensure that
their weight losses did not exceed 1%. Afterwards, the
ingots were cut into several parts and then sealed in
evacuated quartz tubes backﬁlled with pure argon and
annealed at 1073 and 973 K for 90 and 120 days, respec-
tively. After annealing, the samples in quartz tubes were
quickly removed from the furnaces and quenched in an ice-
water mixture.
Finally, the annealed diffusion triples and equilibrated
alloys were characterized using optical microscopy (OM)
and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Quantitative
composition analysis was carried out on a JEOL JXA-
8800R EPMA with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a
probe current of 20 nA. In addition, the phase structure in
some typical alloys were conﬁrmed using x-ray diffraction

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Isothermal section at 1073 K
Figure 3(a) shows the backscattered electron (BSE) image
of the Cu–Cr–Ti diffusion triple annealed at 1073 K for 30
days. The phases and their relative locations in the diffu-
sion region are schematically illustrated in Fig. 3(b), where
each curve stands for an interface between two adjacent
phases, and each tri-junction point represents a three-phase
equilibrium. EPMA line scans were performed across the
phase interfaces within the diffusion zones of the Cu–Cr–
Ti diffusion triples to obtain composition proﬁles, through
which tie-lines between 2 phases were then extracted based
on the assumption of local equilibrium.[33] There is no
intermetallic Cu–Cr phase layer between pure Cu and Cr
interface. A aTiCr2 layer was detected between the end
member Ti and Cr. Because of the interdiffusion of atoms
in the tri-junction of three-metals, aTiCr2 phase layer
extends to the Cu–Cr interface. In addition, six phase layers
formed in the diffusion zone between Cu and Ti bricks.
These layers were identiﬁed to be bTi, CuTi2, CuTi,
Cu4Ti3, Cu3Ti2, and Cu4Ti through EPMA measurements
and referring to the Cu–Ti phase diagram.

Based on the results measured from diffusion triple and
alloy samples in this work, together with the available
information from the binary phase diagrams in literature,
isothermal section of the Cu–Cr–Ti ternary system at
1073 K is constructed in Fig. 6. All the raw composition
data are presented as circles, and tie-lines are shown as
dashed lines in Fig. 6(a). Nine three-phase equilibria were
identiﬁed in the isothermal section, including the two
three-phase equilibria of CuTi ? Cu4Ti3 ? s1 and Cu4-
Ti3 ? s1 ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 which were assumed according to
the trends of the obtained tie-lines of adjacent three two-
phase regions. It is found that the solubility of Cr in Cu3Ti2
is relatively high (about 11.6 at.%), while the other Cu–Ti
intermetallic phases could dissolve very limited Cr (near
1 at.%).
3.2 Isothermal Section at 973 K
Figure 7 shows the diffusion region formed in the Cu–Cr–
Ti diffusion triple annealed at 973 K for 60 days. At
973 K, the Cu3Ti2 phase layer was not observed between
Cu and Ti blocks. However, it should be stable at this
temperature according to phase diagrams of the Cu–Ti
binary system[24] and other related ternary systems.[34, 35] It
maybe that the Cu3Ti2 phase layer is too thin to be deter-
mined in present Cu–Cr–Ti diffusion triple. Another pos-
sibility
is
that
missing
phases
in
diffusion
couples/multiples may occur when equilibrium has not
been obtained. This more likely to occur at lower tem-
peratures where the diffusion kinetics are slow.[33, 36] The
presence of cracks and holes in the diffusion region makes
it difﬁcult to accurately deﬁne the phase equilibria
involving s1 in this work.
To reveal more phase equilibrium relations of the Cu–
Cr–Ti ternary system, 16 typical alloy samples were further
fabricated and annealed at 973 K for 120 days. The con-
stituent phases and compositions in all these equilibrated
alloys were measured by XRD and EPMA, and the results
are summarized in Table 3.
The BSE images and XRD patterns of alloys B10
(Cu52Cr12Ti36), B3 (Cu38Cr18Ti44), B9 (Cu42Cr19Ti39), and
B7 (Cu60Cr4Ti36) annealed at 973 K for 120 days are
shown in Figs. 8 and 9. Figure 8(a) illustrates that alloy
B10 consists of light Cu4Ti phase, gray (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 phase
and dark aTiCr2 phase, which corresponds well with the
XRD results presented in Fig. 9(a). Figures 8(b) and
9(b) indicate that alloy B3 consists of CuTi, aTiCr2 and s1
phases. Furthermore, as presented in Figs. 8(c) and 9(c),
three-phase equilibrium of (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 ? s1 ? aTiCr2
exists in alloy B9. According to Fig. 8(d), alloy B7 is
located in Cu4Ti ? Cu4Ti3 ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 three-phase
region. As mentioned above, the Cu3Ti2 binary phase was
not observed in the diffusion zone of the Cu–Cr–Ti diffu-
sion triple annealed at 973 K. To obtain more information
about this phase region, alloy Cu57.5Cr1.5Ti41 was annealed
at 973 and 1073 K for 120 and 90 days, respectively.
Figure 10(a)–(d) display BSE images and XRD patterns
of alloy Cu57.5Cr1.5Ti41 after annealing at 973 K for
120 days and 1073 K for 90 days, respectively. As shown
in Fig. 10(a), (b), the Cu4Ti and Cu4Ti3 phases were
detected in alloy Cu57.5Cr1.5Ti41 after being annealed at
973 K for 120 days. However, phase Cu3Ti2 was not
identiﬁed in alloy Cu57.5Cr1.5Ti41 annealed at 973 K.
A few researchers[37–39] insist that the Cu3Ti2 phase is
observed at temperatures near 973 K. In Liu’s work,[40] the
Cu3Ti2 phase also was not found at 973 K in Cu–Ti–Hf
system in either diffusion triple or prepared alloys. In
present work, as shown in Fig. 11, the Cu3Ti2 phase was
conﬁrmed that it is stable at 973 K in alloy B16 (Cu60Ti40),
but this binary intermetallic phase was not found in the
ternary alloy B15 (Cu57.5Cr1.5Ti41) which contained two-
phase equilibrium Cu4Ti ? Cu4Ti3. According to the bin-
ary phase diagram of Cu–Ti system, Cu3Ti2 forms through
a peritectoid reaction Cu2Ti ? Cu4Ti3 ? Cu3Ti2, which
needs to overcome high diffusion barrier. Maybe, in both
Cu–Hf–Ti and Cu–Cr–Ti system, the competition of mul-
tiphase formation decelerates the process of peritectoid
reaction, leading to a small fraction or even the absence of
Cu3Ti2. Additional work is needed to conﬁrm the presence
of Cu3Ti2 in ternary system. Longer anneal times are
suggested to overcome any potential sluggish diffusion
kinetics or nucleation issues.

4 Comparison of Phase Equilibrium Relations
at 1073 and 973 K
A preliminary comparison of phase equilibrium relations at
1073 and 973 K, Figs. 6 and 12, shows there are some
evident differences, which are worthy of further discussion.
The solubility of Cr in Cu increases with temperature, from
1.39 at.% at 973 K to 2.2 at.% at 1073 K. Cr dissolves in
Cu3Ti2 at both 1073 and 973 K with a relatively high
solubility of about 12 at.%. (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 and Cu3Ti2 can
form a continuous solid solution at 1073 K, but is phase
separated at 973 K. When the temperature decreases from

Cr)3Ti2 and Cu4Ti3 ? (Cu, Cr)3Ti2 transforms to the three-

5 Crystallographic Information of the s1 Phase
To study the crystallographic information of the (Cu,
Cr)4Ti3 (s1) phase, a few alloys have been prepared.
Though the pure alloy with single s1 phase has not been
obtained, we obtained an alloy sample mainly consisting of
s1. Figure S1 presents the XRD patterns of alloy Cu46-
Cr13Ti41 prepared at 1173 K. By virtue of preliminary
indexing of reﬂections, the s1 phase belongs to tetragonal
structure with the space group of P4/nnc. a = b = 3.109(3),
c = 2.854(3). Of course, the detailed crystallographic
information of s1 remains to be further studied in future
work.
